## Section 1: How Heat Pumps Work (And Why They're Better Than Furnaces)

### The Basics

A heat pump is like a refrigerator in reverse. Instead of pushing cold air out, it pulls heat from outside air (even cold air contains heat) and moves it inside your home.

In summer, it flips: it pulls heat from inside and pushes it outside (AC mode).

**Why this matters:** Heat pumps use electricity way more efficiently than furnaces use gas. A furnace burns fuel and turns it into heat (roughly 80–95% efficient). A heat pump moves existing heat using electricity (300–400% efficient in mild climates, lower in cold climates but still better than furnace).

Translation: Same comfort, way lower bills.

### Heat Pump vs. Furnace: The Numbers

| Factor | Furnace | Heat Pump |
|---|---|---|
| **Upfront Cost** | $4–7K | $4–7K |
| **Annual Heating Cost** | $1,200–1,800 | $600–900 |
| **Annual Cooling Cost** | Not included | Included (mini-split or central) |
| **Lifespan** | 15–20 years | 15–20 years |
| **Maintenance** | Annual service | Less frequent |
| **BC Hydro Rebate** | $0 | $4,000–$8,000 |
| **Net Upfront Cost** | $4–7K | $0–3K (after rebate) |
| **Payback Period** | N/A | 8–12 years |

**Bottom line:** Same upfront cost, way lower operating cost, and BC Hydro pays you $4–8K to switch.

### The Island Grid Factor (Important for Victoria)

Victoria's on the island. Our grid is smaller and gets stressed on cold winter mornings when everyone's heating.

BC Hydro has a solution: Peak Saver.

Peak Saver is a program where you let them pre-heat your home at off-peak times, and during peak demand (winter mornings), your system temporarily reduces output. In exchange, you get paid.

**You don't have to participate.** But most Victoria homeowners do because the incentive is real: $200 one-time + $15–30/month during winter.

Heat pumps are perfect for Peak Saver because they can heat quickly at night (when electricity is cheaper) and coast through peak hours with less draw.

### Typical Scenario

**Home profile:** 
- 2–3 bedroom, single-family home
- Current heating: 20-year-old furnace
- Current annual heating bill: $1,500
- Interest: Heat pump only (solar separate conversation)

**After heat pump installation:**
- Annual heating cost: $700–900
- Annual savings: $600–800
- Peak Saver enrollment: +$200/year
- **Total annual savings: $800–1,000**

**Over 10 years:** $8,000–$10,000 saved on heating alone.

Plus, you get the rebate upfront ($4–8K), which makes the system cost close to zero.

### For Baseboard Heater Users

If your home has electric baseboard heating (common in older Victoria homes):

- Current annual heating cost: $2,000–2,800
- After heat pump: $700–900
- **Annual savings: $1,100–1,900**
- Payback period: 4–6 years (vs. 8–12 for furnace users)

Baseboard heaters are incredibly inefficient. Heat pump payback is fastest for you.

### If You Add Solar Later

If you install solar after your heat pump:

- Heat pump + solar combo covers ~90% of annual energy needs
- You're basically off-grid in terms of energy cost
- Payback accelerates to 6–8 years
- Long-term savings (25-year roof lifespan): $40,000+

## Section 3: Rebates Available to Victoria Homeowners

### BC Hydro Heat Pump Rebate

**Amount:** $4,000–$8,000 (depends on system type)

**Eligibility:**
- Your home must be on BC Hydro's service territory (Victoria is ✓)
- System must be installed by HPCN-certified installer ✓
- New installation (not replacing another heat pump)

**What's included:**
- Air-source heat pumps: $4,000–$5,000
- Ductless mini-split: $5,000–$6,000 (more expensive to install)
- Ground-source (geothermal): $6,000–$8,000 (if you dig)

**How to claim:**
- Installer submits application
- BC Hydro inspects system
- Rebate processes 30–45 days after inspection

**Note:** This is BC Hydro's program. We help with paperwork, but the timeline is outside our control.

### Peak Saver Enrollment Bonus

**Amount:** $200 one-time bonus + $15–30/month during winter

**What it is:**
- BC Hydro's demand response program
- You enroll your heat pump
- They pre-heat your home during off-peak hours
- During peak demand (cold winter mornings), they reduce output slightly
- In exchange, you get paid

**How much does it reduce output?**
- Not enough to notice comfort change
- Think: instead of heating to 72°F, they heat to 71°F, then coast
- Modern heat pumps have smart controls to handle this

**Is it worth it?**
- Yes. $200 + $200–360/year is real money
- You're not sacrificing comfort (1–2°F reduction is imperceptible)
- You're helping the island grid stay stable in winter
- In exchange, you get paid

**Enrollment:**
- Automatic when you install (installer handles it)
- Can opt out if you prefer (but most people don't)

## Section 4: Peak Saver Demand Response Program Explained

### Why BC Hydro Created Peak Saver

**The problem:** Victoria's island grid is small. On cold January mornings when it's 5°C and everyone's heating, the grid can get stressed. BC Hydro has to buy expensive power from other regions.

**Their solution:** Instead of building new infrastructure, they incentivize homeowners to shift heating loads. Pre-heat at night (cheap), coast at morning peak (expensive). Everyone wins.

### How Peak Saver Works with Your Heat Pump

**Setup:**
- Installer connects your heat pump to a smart control
- You enroll (online, takes 2 min)
- BC Hydro monitors your system

**What happens:**
- Most days: Your heat pump operates normally
- Peak hours (typically 7am–10am, Dec–Feb): BC Hydro sends a signal
- Your system reduces heating output by ~1–2°F
- You don't notice the difference
- Peak hours last 2–4 hours on maybe 20–30 days per year

**What you get:**
- $200 bonus in year 1 (just for enrolling)
- $15–30/month during peak months (Dec–Feb)
- Annual total: $200–$360 depending on participation

### Is It Worth It?

**Comfort impact:** Minimal. You won't notice 1–2°F reduction for a few hours.

**Financial impact:** $200–360/year is real money, especially combined with rebates.

**Grid impact:** You're helping the island grid stay stable. When enough homes shift demand, BC Hydro doesn't have to buy expensive peaker power.

**My take:** Everyone we've talked to considers it a win. You get paid to help the grid. Hard to pass up.

## Section 5: System Types — Air-Source vs. Mini-Split vs. Ground-Source

### Air-Source Heat Pump (Most Common)

**How it works:** Outdoor unit pulls heat from air, indoor furnace-style distribution through ductwork.

**Best for:** Homes with existing ductwork (your furnace is already ducted).

**Cost:** $4,500–$6,500 (installed) → $500–$2,500 after rebate

**Pros:**
- Lower upfront cost
- Works with existing ductwork
- Central heating/cooling throughout home
- Easier maintenance

**Cons:**
- Needs ductwork (retrofitting can be expensive)
- Furnace replacement is required

**Victoria suitability:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (most homes have furnaces with ducts)

### Ductless Mini-Split (Zoned Heating)

**How it works:** Outdoor unit + multiple indoor wall units (no ductwork needed). Zones can heat independently.

**Best for:** 
- Homes without ductwork
- Historic homes where duct installation is difficult
- Homes where you want zone control (master bedroom warm, living room cooler)

**Cost:** $7,000–$9,000 (installed) → $1,000–$4,000 after rebate

**Pros:**
- No ductwork needed (huge for Victoria's older homes)
- Zone control (heat only the rooms you're using)
- More efficient than ducted in some scenarios
- Cooler/quieter operation

**Cons:**
- Higher upfront cost
- Wall units are visible (not hidden in walls)
- Zone control requires discipline (or you leave all on)

**Victoria suitability:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (many older Victoria homes lack ducts)

### Ground-Source / Geothermal (Premium)

**How it works:** Loops buried underground pull heat from ground (geothermal). Highly efficient because ground temp is stable.

**Best for:** 
- Homeowners who want maximum efficiency
- Large homes where monthly savings justify cost
- Homes with yard space for drilling/loops

**Cost:** $18,000–$25,000 (installed) → $10,000–$17,000 after rebate

**Pros:**
- Most efficient system (300–500% efficiency)
- Lowest operating costs long-term
- Quieter (no outdoor unit noise)
- Highest rebate available

**Cons:**
- Very high upfront cost
- Requires drilling/ground preparation
- Needs qualified installer (fewer in Victoria)
- Payback takes longer (12–15 years) despite efficiency

**Victoria suitability:** ⭐⭐ (high cost + soft ground needs special drilling)

### Victoria's Recommendation

**Most Victoria homes:** Air-source (ductwork exists) or mini-split (for older homes without ducts).

**Why:** Both are efficient, affordable, and have strong installers in Victoria. Ground-source is overkill for most homes given upfront cost.

**Ask your installer:** They'll assess your existing system and recommend the best fit.

## Section 6: Installation Timeline — What to Expect

### Pre-Installation (Week 1–2)

**What happens:**
- You complete our rebate calculator
- You get matched with 2-3 installers
- Each sends a detailed proposal (same format, easy to compare)
- You ask questions, compare pricing
- You choose your installer and sign contract

**Your timeline:** 1–2 weeks (your pace)

### Paperwork Phase (Week 3)

**What happens:**
- Installer files BC Hydro rebate application
- Installer files federal tax credit documentation
- Peak Saver enrollment forms submitted
- Building permit check (if required by city)

**You:** Provide ID, proof of residence, insurance info

**Installer:** Handles the paperwork (this is their job)

**Typical issues:** None, if installer is experienced

### Installation Day(s) (Week 4–5)

**Timeline:** Typically 1–2 days depending on system type

**Air-source system:**
- Day 1: Remove old furnace, install outdoor + indoor units, connect ductwork
- Day 2: Final connections, testing, system startup

**Mini-split system:**
- Day 1: Install outdoor unit, interior wall units, refrigerant lines
- Day 2: Electrical connections, testing, smart controls setup

**What to expect:**
- Noise (drilling, cutting, equipment)
- Temporary disruption (heating off for a few hours)
- Installer + helper on-site
- About 4–8 hours per day

**You:** Provide access, ask questions, familiarize yourself with controls

### Inspection Phase (Week 6)

**What happens:**
- BC Hydro inspector visits (they call first to schedule)
- They verify system is installed correctly
- Inspection takes ~1 hour
- Inspector signs off = rebate processing begins

**You:** Just be home; inspector does the work

**Timeline:** BC Hydro typically inspects within 2 weeks of installation request

### Rebate Processing (Week 7–10)

**What happens:**
- BC Hydro processes rebate application
- Typical timeline: 30–45 days after inspection
- Rebate arrives as check or utility credit
- Federal tax credit: You claim on next tax return

**You:** Wait (meanwhile, you're already saving on heating bills)

### Total Timeline

- Best case: 6 weeks (if everything moves quickly)
- Typical case: 8 weeks (more realistic)
- Worst case: 12 weeks (if BC Hydro is backed up)

**Note:** The 30–45 day BC Hydro timeline is outside our control, but it's their standard. Most process within this window.

## Section 8: How to Maximize Your Rebate

### Before You Apply

**1. Get the rebate amounts confirmed**
- These amounts change. Verify with BC Hydro directly.
- Most installers know current amounts, but double-check.

**2. Ensure your installer is HPCN certified**
- Rebate requires certified installer.
- All our Victoria installers are. ✓

**3. Understand what system type qualifies**
- Air-source, mini-split, ground-source all qualify.
- Some rebates depend on system efficiency (SEER rating).
- Installer will ensure you qualify.

### During Installation

**4. Get written confirmation of all work**
- Install invoice should itemize everything
- Keep receipts for tax credit claim

**5. Document the system details**
- Serial numbers
- Installation date
- Efficiency ratings (SEER, HSPF)
- Needed for rebate application

### After Installation

**6. Ensure BC Hydro inspection happens**
- Installer schedules this
- Inspection = rebate processing begins
- Don't skip this step (some people do—don't be that person)

**7. File federal tax credit**
- Keep all receipts
- Claim on next year's tax return
- Or have your CPA file (they know the form)

**8. Enroll in Peak Saver**
- Automatic, but verify enrollment
- Free $200 + monthly credits
- Easy money

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

❌ **Installing before research** → Pick wrong system type → Miss rebate eligibility

❌ **Hiring non-certified installer** → No rebate (program requires HPCN)

❌ **Not documenting the work** → Can't claim federal credit if you don't have receipts

❌ **Skipping BC Hydro inspection** → Rebate never processes

❌ **Not signing up for Peak Saver** → Missing $200–360/year

## Section 9: Common Questions

### Q: Will the heat pump heat my entire home?

**A:** Yes. Air-source and ground-source systems heat your whole home. Mini-split systems heat all rooms you install wall units in (most people do all bedrooms + living areas). The installer will size the system for your home's heating needs.

### Q: Does it work if it's really cold outside?

**A:** Yes. Victoria's winters aren't that cold (average low ~5°C). Modern cold-climate heat pumps work down to -15°C. You're fine. (If you were in Fort St. John, we'd say different—much colder there.)

### Q: Can I keep my gas furnace as backup?

**A:** You can (some people do), but it's not necessary. Heat pumps are reliable. If you're worried, install a small backup electric heater. But most people just have the heat pump. Cost to keep furnace: expensive ($$ for gas line maintenance, takes up space).

### Q: Do heat pumps make noise?

**A:** Outdoor unit is about as loud as an AC unit—noticeable but not loud. Indoor unit is very quiet. You won't hear it running.

### Q: Can I install a heat pump myself?

**A:** No. Requires HPCN certification, refrigerant handling license, electrical permit, building permit in many cases. Must be professional. Plus, rebate requires certified installer anyway.

### Q: What if I rent my home?

**A:** Rebate goes to the property owner (you), not tenant. But you own it now so you're good. If you eventually sell, the new owner inherits the benefit (lower energy costs).

### Q: How long do heat pumps last?

**A:** 15–20 years, same as furnaces. Maintenance is less frequent (service every 2 years vs. furnace annual). Long-term reliability is good.

### Q: What if BC Hydro rebate program ends?

**A:** It might eventually, but that's outside your control. Your rebate is locked in at installation. Federal tax credit changes slowly (Congress would need to vote). Don't let "what if" paralysis stop you from saving money now.
