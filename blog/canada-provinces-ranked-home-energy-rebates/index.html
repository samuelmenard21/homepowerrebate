Canada's Home Energy Rebates, Ranked by Province

We build rebate guides for a living, which means we've spent months inside every province's fine print. Some provinces are genuinely trying to help homeowners electrify. Others have basically given up. Here's the ranking, with the real numbers behind it.

By Sam Menard · Updated August 2026 · 12 min read

How we ranked these

Three things: total realistic rebate stack for a typical home doing a heat pump + insulation + solar project, program coherence (one clear program vs. a scavenger hunt across five agencies), and whether the money is actually flowing right now, not a program that closed six months ago but still ranks on Google.

We're not ranking population or climate policy ambition. We're ranking what a homeowner can actually put in their pocket in 2026.

Atlantic provinces beyond Nova Scotia (New Brunswick, PEI, Newfoundland) and the territories don't have developed enough programs to rank meaningfully as of 2026 — mostly limited to federal-only coverage.

The breakdown

#1 British Columbia
~$35,000 stack

CleanBC isn't just generous, it's coherent. One heat pump rebate ($4,000–$16,000), one solar rebate ($5,000), one insulation program ($5,500), one windows program ($2,000), one water heater rebate ($3,000). A homeowner can understand the whole system in an afternoon. That matters as much as the dollar figure — a $30K stack nobody can navigate is worth less than a $20K stack anyone can use.

BC is the only province where we'd say the program design itself, not just the funding level, is best-in-class. And it's not just heat pumps — battery storage ($5,000), EV chargers (up to $550), and smart thermostats ($150–$300) are all part of the same coherent system.

Fun fact: Kelowna runs on FortisBC gas instead of BC Hydro electricity — a different utility than most of the province — which changes the rebate math enough that it gets its own separate calculation on our Kelowna page.
#2 Quebec
$15,000–$20,000+ stack

This one surprised us. Quebec's solar rebate through Hydro-Québec pays $1,000/kW up to 20kW — a 10kW system nets roughly $10,000, which is double BC's flat $5,000 rebate. Heat pumps through LogisVert go up to $6,700 (air-source) or $10,000 (geothermal). Insulation through Rénoclimat adds up to $3,750 more.

Two real issues keep Quebec out of first place. First, it's split across two separate programs with different rules, which is real friction compared to BC's single-program design. Second — and this is the practical reason most rebate-guide sites skip Quebec entirely — the province's Charter of the French Language legally requires French content for anything marketing to Quebec consumers, with real enforcement (fines starting at $3,000/day). That's not a knock on the rebate program itself, which is genuinely strong. It's just why you'll see far less English-language coverage of Quebec's rebates than the numbers deserve.

Fun fact: Quebec is the only province on this list where solar pays more the bigger your system gets — every other province's solar rebate (where one exists) is a flat number regardless of system size.
#3 Ontario
$15,000–$25,000 stack (varies widely)

Ontario's Home Renovation Savings Program pays real money — up to $2,000/ton for cold-climate heat pumps, $100 per window opening, $500/ton for water heaters. The problem is consistency. Amounts shift depending on your fuel type, your city, and sometimes your specific utility territory. Two homes ten minutes apart can qualify for meaningfully different amounts.

Ontario also has the largest population of any province we cover (15.4M), so the sheer number of homeowners who benefit outweighs some of the structural messiness. It's a strong program buried under an inconsistent structure — insulation, water heaters, EV chargers, and smart thermostats all have their own line items too, not just heat pumps.

Fun fact: Ontario is the only province on this list where the rebate is tiered by heating fuel type rather than income — a home switching off oil gets a different number than one switching off natural gas, even in the same city.
#4 Nova Scotia
$5,000–$15,000 stack

Efficiency Nova Scotia used to run some of the most aggressive stackable grants and interest-free loans in the country. That era is largely over: the core Home Heating System Rebates Program closed to new applicants on December 31, 2025, and the federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability Program closed July 2, 2026. What survives is real but thinner — up to $5,000 for any homeowner, up to $15,000 for moderate-income households, and free installation for income-qualified homes through HomeWarming.

Halifax adds its own wrinkle worth knowing: the city runs Solar City, property-assessed solar financing at 4.75% fixed over 10 years billed through your property tax, on top of the provincial $0.60/watt rebate. It's financing, not free money, but it removes the upfront cash barrier that stops a lot of solar projects.

Nova Scotia is a province in decline on this metric, not because homeowners stopped needing help, but because the funding did. Insulation, air sealing, and smart thermostats are still free, and water heaters still get up to $2,500 — the collapse is specific to the flagship heat pump program, not the whole system.

Fun fact: Nova Scotia moved first — it closed its flagship rebate before any other province, which is part of why so much "current" rebate content online is quietly out of date.
#5 Manitoba
$250–$2,500 stack

Manitoba's heat pump rebate ranges from $250 to $2,000 depending on category, with an extra $500 for income-qualified households. That's a fraction of what BC or Quebec offer for the identical equipment. The one exception was the federal Oil to Heat Pump program, which could add up to $20,000 combined for homes switching off oil heat — but that program also stopped taking new applications after July 31, 2026, leaving Manitoba back to its thin baseline. Solar, battery, and EV charger rebates are effectively absent provincially.

Fun fact: Manitoba's utility rebate structure categorizes heat pumps by type rather than a flat rate, which is why the same technology can qualify for anywhere from $250 to $2,000 depending on which box it fits into.
#6 Alberta
$2,000–$5,000 stack

Alberta has no provincial rebate program for home energy upgrades. What it has is the Clean Energy Improvement Program (CEIP) — which is financing, not a rebate, a low-interest loan attached to your property tax bill, sometimes with a small incentive on top. Utility rebates from ENMAX and ATCO run $500 to $2,000 per unit and are described by installers as changing "throughout the year," which is not a confidence-inspiring way to plan a $12,000 purchase. There's no solar rebate, no battery rebate, and no EV charger rebate in Alberta at all.

The clearest sign of how thin this is: as of January 2026, the federal Oil to Heat Pump program — Albertans' best available option for heat pump help — had received just 16 applications and issued a single grant — province-wide, not per city.

Some cities do better than the provincial baseline. Calgary runs a free Home Upgrades Program for income-qualified residents (currently waitlisted). Lethbridge, which has its own municipal electric utility, offers a small $400 environmental incentive. St. Albert adds a $1,400 cash bonus on top of CEIP. Red Deer brought back a $50 smart thermostat rebate. None of it changes the province's overall ranking, but it means the city you live in matters more in Alberta than almost anywhere else in Canada.

For a province with Alberta's population and home values, this is a real gap, not a rounding error. Full breakdown, all 8 categories: Alberta Home Energy Rebates.

Fun fact: As of January 2026, the federal Oil to Heat Pump program had issued exactly 1 grant in all of Alberta — read the full story: Alberta's Heat Pump Program Got 16 Applications.
#7 Saskatchewan
~$0 for heat pumps

Saskatchewan is last, and it's not close. There is currently no heat pump rebate in the province at all. SaskPower's Home Efficiency Retrofit Rebate covers building-envelope work — windows, doors, insulation, air sealing — up to $1,800, but heat pumps specifically are excluded. If you're switching off oil heat, the federal Oil to Heat Pump program is (or was, before it closed) your only real option.

A homeowner in Saskatchewan replacing a furnace with a heat pump today gets essentially the same support as someone doing nothing at all. Solar, battery, water heater, and smart thermostat rebates are also absent — the $1,800 envelope program is genuinely the entire toolkit.

Fun fact: Saskatchewan's $1,800 envelope rebate technically covers doors and windows — the exact categories that qualify a homeowner for nothing on the heating side.

What this means if you're deciding where to invest

If you're a homeowner: BC and Quebec are where your rebate dollars go furthest. Ontario's money is real but requires more homework to find. Everywhere else, budget for the upgrade as if the rebate is a bonus, not a plan.

If you're a policymaker reading this in Regina or Edmonton: the gap between what BC/Quebec offer and what Alberta/Saskatchewan offer isn't a rounding error. It's the difference between a homeowner switching to a heat pump this year and one waiting another decade.
