12 ways to cut your energy bill in Massachusetts

Mass Save's budget got cut by $500 million in 2025, and the impact is already showing up in 2026 rebate ceilings. Here's what changed, what didn't, and what still works regardless.

By Sam Menard · Updated August 2026 · 9 min read

1. Know the real Mass Save ceiling before you plan around a bigger number

Massachusetts's Department of Public Utilities cut $500 million from Mass Save's proposed three-year budget in early 2025, reducing total residential program funding by 25% for gas and 15% for electric. The direct effect for 2026: the maximum heat pump rebate dropped from $10,000 to $8,500. If you've seen the old $10,000 figure referenced anywhere, confirm the current ceiling with your contractor before budgeting around it. (Massachusetts DPU press release)

2. Apply early and keep every piece of paperwork

Mass Save's rebate processing has run 8-12 weeks behind in 2024-2026, and the program brought in an outside company specifically to clear the backlog. File your application as early as possible in the project, and keep copies of every invoice, model number, and contractor form — a missing document is the most common reason a rebate check gets delayed further.

3. Confirm your contractor is a Mass Save network participant

The 0% HEAT Loan (up to $25,000, 7-year term) and most rebates route through your contractor's own paperwork rather than a separate application. If your contractor isn't a participating installer, you may not be able to access the loan or rebate at all for that job, regardless of the equipment. Ask before you sign, not after.

4. Check that your equipment's refrigerant still qualifies

Some heat pump models using R-410A refrigerant no longer qualify for Mass Save rebates in 2026. A contractor quoting older stock may not realize the model fell off the qualifying list. Confirm eligibility with the specific model number before you sign a contract, not after installation.

5. Look at delivery charges, not just usage, when a bill spikes

Some Massachusetts homeowners have reported bills over $1,000 a month where delivery charges, not the underlying commodity cost, made up the majority of the increase. Eversource and National Grid both run in-person events to help customers decode confusing bills — worth attending before assuming a bill is simply an error or a usage spike.

6. Use the level payment plan if winter bills swing hard

Eversource and National Grid both offer a payment plan that spreads your annual cost evenly across 12 months instead of concentrating it in the coldest months. If your winter bills spike sharply, this doesn't lower the total, but it removes the shock of a single oversized bill.

7. Get a real energy assessment before assuming what your home needs

A Mass Save Home Energy Assessment is still free and still identifies the specific gaps in your specific house, rather than generic advice. Even with a reduced rebate ceiling, the assessment itself remains one of the highest-value free things available to a Massachusetts homeowner.

8. Weigh oil-to-heat-pump conversion against current oil prices

Cold-climate heat pumps have been documented running through New England winters even in homes with an uninsulated attic, and oil-to-heat-pump converts commonly report roughly half the running cost of oil at current prices. Run the comparison against your specific oil delivery cost before assuming a heat pump won't keep up with a Massachusetts winter.

9. Insulate before you size new heating equipment

Massachusetts's older triple-decker and colonial housing stock often has minimal insulation behind the walls. Insulating first, or at the same time as a heat pump swap, lets you size the new equipment smaller and lowers your ongoing heating cost regardless of which system you choose.

10. Shift laundry, dishwasher, and EV charging off-peak where your rate structure allows it

If your utility offers a time-of-use option, moving flexible, schedulable loads to cheaper hours is a free lever that doesn't require any equipment change.

11. Seal and weatherstrip before the heating season, not during it

Door sweeps, outlet gaskets, and frame weatherstripping are the cheapest fix on this list and pay back within a season in a New England climate.

12. Get your real number before you commit

With the Mass Save ceiling now $8,500 instead of $10,000, and a documented processing backlog, the math on any project looks different than it did a year ago. Run your specific address and project through an assessment to see the current, real numbers.
