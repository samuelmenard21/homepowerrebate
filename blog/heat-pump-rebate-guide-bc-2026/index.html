Heat pump rebates in BC, 2026: the complete guide

By Sam Menard · Updated July 2026 · 5 min read

A heat pump is the single most rebated home upgrade in British Columbia — and also the most confusing, because the money comes from three or four programs at once and the amount depends on your income. This is the plain-English map of all of it: what you can get, who pays it, how the pieces stack, and the order to do things in so you don't leave money on the table.

The short version: In 2026, CleanBC heat pump rebates run from $4,000 to $16,000 depending on your household income, plus up to $5,000 for the electrical work — so a switch from gas or baseboard heat can be rebated to the tune of ~$21,000. Income-qualified households can have a heat pump installed for close to free.

The programs that pay for a heat pump

There isn't one "heat pump rebate" in BC. There are several programs that layer on top of each other, delivered through your utility. Here's who pays what:

  • CleanBC Better Homes — the main provincial rebate. This is the big one, and it scales with your household income.
  • Your utility (BC Hydro or FortisBC) — delivers the CleanBC money and sometimes adds top-ups, depending on where you live.
  • Municipal top-ups — a handful of cities (like Vancouver) add their own money on top.
  • Federal programs — funnelled to lower-income households through the new Canada Greener Homes Affordability Program (the old Greener Homes Grant and Loan are both closed).

How much you'll actually get (the income tiers)

The CleanBC heat pump rebate isn't a flat number — it's tiered by household income. The lower your income, the more of the cost is covered. These are the general bands; your exact amount is confirmed by a required EnerGuide home evaluation.

Standard households: a few thousand dollars off, on the lower end of the $4,000–$16,000 range.
Moderate income: enhanced rebates in the middle of the range.
Income-qualified households (for example, a family of four earning up to roughly $57,200): up to $16,000 — often enough to cover the entire installation. This is the "free heat pump" program most people don't know exists.

On top of the heat pump itself, switching off gas or baseboard heat usually means an electrical panel or service upgrade — and there's a separate rebate of up to $5,000 for that. That's how the headline "$21,000" total comes together.

If you heat with oil, gas, or propane in the North

Homes north of 100 Mile House get extra money to get off fossil heat: up to $6,000 to replace oil heating with a heat pump, or up to $8,000 to replace natural gas or propane — on top of the standard CleanBC amounts. If you're in Prince George or Fort St. John, these northern top-ups are the real story.

"But does a heat pump even work in a BC winter?"

Short answer: yes — modern cold-climate heat pumps are rated to heat efficiently well below freezing, and they run all winter across BC, including the Interior and the North. The order of operations (do this, not that)

The single most expensive mistake is buying equipment before you've booked the evaluation. CleanBC requires an EnerGuide home evaluation before your install to lock in eligibility. Here's the sequence:

  1. Check your numbers first. Our free rebate assessment estimates your income tier and total in about a minute.
  2. Book a pre-retrofit EnerGuide evaluation. This is required and it sets your rebate ceiling.
  3. Get the heat pump installed by a program-registered contractor. Choosing well matters — here's how to spot the red flags and why quotes vary so much.
  4. Book the post-retrofit evaluation and file your rebate applications.
  5. Stack the rest. While the crew is there, insulation, a heat-pump water heater, and windows all carry their own rebates and make the heat pump work better.

Stacking: how a heat pump becomes ~$21,000 back

The heat pump is the anchor, but it's most valuable as the centre of a stack. A typical BC retrofit combines the heat pump ($4,000–$16,000), the electrical upgrade (up to $5,000), a heat-pump water heater ($1,000–$3,000), and insulation (up to $5,500). We break down the real math — including where the numbers actually land — in the stacking guide and how to combine BC and federal money.

Which brand should you buy?

For rebates, the brand doesn't change your amount — any program-registered cold-climate model qualifies. It matters for reliability and winter performance, though. See our Mitsubishi vs. Daikin vs. Bosch comparison, and the heat pumps explained primer if you're starting from zero.
