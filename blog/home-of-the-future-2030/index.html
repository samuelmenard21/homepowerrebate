What the home of 2030 actually looks like

Not a concept house — the realistic version, built from where heat pumps, solar, batteries and smart controls are actually headed.

By Sam Menard · August 2026 · 7 min read

Skip the sci-fi version. Based on where the technology and policy trends are actually pointing between now and 2030, here's a realistic walkthrough of a fully-electrified home — and how close (or far) a typical BC house is from it today.

The heating and cooling system: one machine, not two

By 2030, the furnace-plus-AC pairing is the exception, not the rule, in new construction and most retrofits. A cold-climate heat pump handles both jobs from a single outdoor unit. California alone is targeting 6 million installed heat pumps by 2030, and performance in sub-zero conditions keeps closing the gap with the coldest climates. In BC, this part of the future is already arriving — heat pump rebates through CleanBC and BC Hydro remain some of the largest available. See our heat pump explainer for how ducted vs. ductless plays out in different home types.

The roof: generating, not just covering

Solar keeps climbing the global generation ranks — overtaking wind in 2027, then hydropower in 2029 to become the single largest renewable source. Panel efficiency keeps improving, and by 2030 renewables are projected to supply 46% of global electricity, with solar and wind together making up 30%. On an individual home, that translates to solar becoming a default line item in new-build budgets rather than an optional upsell, the same way double-glazed windows became standard rather than a luxury feature over the past few decades.

The garage or utility room: a battery, not just a panel

This is the fastest-moving piece. Battery costs have fallen more than 2x in two years and more than 3x in three. Sodium-ion batteries reach residential markets by late 2027; solid-state batteries follow in 2028–2029, both offering better safety margins and lower costs than today's lithium-ion. By 2030, integrated battery storage is expected to be a standard feature in new-build homes — the same shift that took central air conditioning from optional to assumed. If you're buying a battery today, see our guide on choosing a BC-approved battery before the rebate list changes.

Why the battery matters more than people expect

A heat pump and solar panels each solve one problem. A battery is what makes the other two actually work together — storing midday solar for the evening heating load, and riding out peak-pricing windows instead of paying for them. It's the piece most homeowners underrate when planning a retrofit in sequence.

The driveway: charging is part of the electrical plan, not an afterthought

EV charging keeps climbing as a driver of household electricity demand alongside heat pumps — both are named directly in the leading 2026 electrification outlooks as the two forces reshaping home electrical design. A 2030 home's panel, wiring and even its solar/battery sizing get planned around charging load from day one, rather than requiring a retrofit later. BC homeowners can already get ahead of this with the EV charger rebate and Time-of-Day rate guide.

The thermostat and panel: a system, not separate gadgets

Smart thermostats stop being a $200 add-on and become the control layer that makes the rest of the system worth its cost — shifting heat pump runtime off peak pricing windows, coordinating with the battery, and adjusting for whichever utility program the home is enrolled in that year. What doesn't change: you still need to sequence it right

None of this arrives all at once, and buying the pieces in the wrong order wastes money. Insulation and air-sealing still come first — a leaky envelope makes every system above oversized and underperforming, which is exactly why we wrote insulation-first up in detail. After that, heat pump, then solar, then battery is the sequence that tends to pay back fastest, because each layer makes the next one smaller and cheaper.

The takeaway

The 2030 home isn't exotic — it's a heat pump, solar, a battery, EV-ready wiring, and a smart thermostat coordinating all four. Most of that is buildable today, several years ahead of "standard," while rebates are still open and before installer demand catches up with the trend.
