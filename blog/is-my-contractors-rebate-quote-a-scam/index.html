Is My Contractor's Rebate Quote a Scam?

A contractor quotes $25,000 and says a rebate will cover $20,000 of it. That math should make you pause — real rebate program caps are almost never that high. Here's how to check before you sign.

By Sam Menard · August 2026 · 6 min read

The pattern to watch for

This is one of the most common ways homeowners end up overpaying for a heat pump, solar system, or insulation job. It works like this: a contractor quotes a price well above what the job should cost, then justifies it by claiming a rebate will cover most of the difference. The final "net" number sounds reasonable, so homeowners sign. Later, the rebate that actually comes through is a fraction of what was promised — because the program's real cap was never anywhere near that high.

A commonly cited example: a contractor quotes $25,000 for a job, tells the homeowner $20,000 of that comes back through rebates, and presents the "real" cost as $5,000. In reality, most heat pump, solar, and insulation rebate programs cap out well below $20,000 per project — often in the low thousands, occasionally reaching into five figures only for the largest income-qualified programs. If a contractor's promised rebate is close to matching the whole job cost, that's the number to check before anything else.

The tell: A quote that only "works" because of a huge promised rebate is a quote built backward from a number the contractor wants you to pay, not from the real cost of the job.

Why this works on homeowners

Rebate programs are genuinely confusing. Amounts vary by utility, by income, by upgrade type, and they change throughout the year as funding cycles open and close. Most homeowners have no easy way to verify a number a contractor states verbally, so they trust the person standing in their kitchen. A dishonest or simply careless contractor can quote almost any rebate figure and the homeowner has no quick way to check it — until the real rebate comes back much lower than promised, months after the contract is signed.

How to verify the real cap before you sign

  1. Get the exact program name in writing. Not "rebates" or "government programs" — the specific program, like your utility's heat pump rebate or your state/province's solar incentive.
  2. Look it up yourself on the official source. Go to your utility's website or your state/provincial energy office directly — never rely on a number relayed by the contractor, and never use a link the contractor sends you without checking the domain is the real utility or government site.
  3. Check the dollar cap for your specific upgrade. Most programs publish a maximum rebate per unit or per project (for example, "$X per ton of heating capacity" or "up to $X for a qualifying system"). Compare that number directly to what the contractor promised.
  4. Check who's eligible. Some of the largest rebate amounts are reserved for income-qualified households. Confirm you actually meet the income or other eligibility requirements before counting on the top-tier number.
  5. Ask what happens if the rebate is denied or lower than expected. Get this answer in writing. A contract that leaves you owing the full inflated price regardless of the rebate outcome is a bad contract.
  6. Get a second quote. If a second contractor quotes a similar job for meaningfully less, without leaning on a rebate to make the math work, that's a strong signal the first quote was inflated.

A rough gut-check table

Real rebate caps vary a lot by program and region — always verify your specific number — but this gives a general sense of scale to sanity-check a quote against:

UpgradeTypical published rebate range
Heat pump (standard utility rebate)Roughly $500 – $9,000, depending on system size and utility
Heat pump (income-qualified, largest programs)Up to around $8,000 – $10,000 in the most generous programs
Solar PVMostly a tax credit or per-kWh incentive, not a flat cash rebate, in most regions today
Insulation / air sealingRoughly $200 – $2,000 depending on scope and utility
Battery storageVaries widely; some regions offer per-kWh incentives worth several thousand dollars

These are general ranges to sanity-check a quote against, not a substitute for looking up your exact program. Always confirm the real number for your city, utility, and income tier on the program's official page — see your region's rebate guide for current, sourced figures.

If a promised rebate is close to matching the total job cost, verify it before you sign — not after. Once the contract is signed at the inflated price, you're the one holding the risk if the real rebate comes back lower.

Is it ever normal for a contractor to handle the rebate?

Yes. Many legitimate point-of-sale rebates are designed so the contractor applies the discount at checkout and handles the paperwork for you — that's a real convenience, not a scam by itself. The difference is transparency: a legitimate contractor will name the exact program and put the rebate amount in writing before you sign. A contractor who won't name the program, or who gives you a verbal number that conveniently makes an inflated price look affordable, is the one worth being cautious about.
