Is the Person at My Door Really From the Rebate Program?

Real police and utility warnings from Nova Scotia and real consumer complaints from Alberta show the same pattern: someone at your door claiming to represent a rebate program, asking for documents that unlock a loan in your name.

By Sam Menard · August 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

Probably not, if they showed up unannounced. Rebate programs like Efficiency Nova Scotia have said publicly that they don't cold-call or door-knock homeowners who haven't already applied. If someone at your door says they're "from the rebate program" or "from the utility" and wants to move fast — get you to sign something, hand over a document, or book an install today — slow down and verify independently before you do anything.

The core red flag: A legitimate rebate program never needs to knock on your door to give you money. You apply to them, not the other way around.

What's actually been happening in Nova Scotia

Halifax Regional Police and Efficiency Nova Scotia have both issued public warnings about door-to-door and cold-call sales reps falsely claiming an affiliation with Efficiency Nova Scotia to sell heat pumps. The pattern reported: a salesperson shows up, says they're connected to the program, and promises a rebate that will cover most or all of the cost. Homeowners sign up for financing on the spot — often at high interest rates — on the understanding the rebate will cover the payments. In some cases the rebate application was never properly submitted, was incomplete, or the homeowner didn't actually qualify, and they're left holding a loan they didn't expect.

Efficiency Nova Scotia's own guidance is direct: it will not contact you out of the blue unless you've already applied to one of its programs. If you didn't reach out to them first, an unsolicited visit or call claiming otherwise should be treated with suspicion.

Sources: CBC News — "'Predatory' companies selling heat pumps by making false rebate promises, warns Efficiency N.S.", Nova Scotia Crime Stoppers — Halifax Regional Police warning, Efficiency Nova Scotia — official notice

Why they want your Notice of Assessment and utility bill

These two documents aren't random paperwork — they're exactly what a lender needs to approve a home-improvement loan quickly, without you fully understanding what you signed. Your Notice of Assessment (the summary the tax agency sends you) proves your income. Your utility bill proves your address and, sometimes, your energy usage. Handing both to someone at your door, before you've verified who they actually work for, can put a loan application in motion using your name and your credit.

If you're ever asked for these documents by someone who approached you first, stop and verify their identity through an official channel before handing anything over.

What happened in Alberta: the Sunwave Solar complaints

A related but different pattern showed up in Alberta. Global News reported that homeowners in Okotoks and elsewhere, including Larry Swanson, were approached by door-to-door salespeople from a company called Sunwave Solar. Swanson paid a $3,500 deposit toward a $10,100 solar install after being told he'd get roughly $3,600 back through the federal Canada Greener Homes Grant. Months later, he said, he hadn't heard back from the company and hadn't received the installation. Several other Alberta homeowners reported similar experiences — deposits paid, rebates promised, no product delivered. The federal government opened an investigation into consumer complaints against the company.

This isn't identical to the Nova Scotia pattern — it's deposit money at risk rather than a fraudulent loan — but the setup is the same: a door-to-door pitch, a promised rebate used to justify signing quickly, and pressure to commit before you've had a chance to check anything independently.

Source: Global News — "Sunwave Solar Okotoks company complaint" and Global News — federal investigation follow-up

How to check before you sign anything

  • Get the salesperson's full name and company name in writing before they leave.
  • Don't sign a contract, financing agreement, or hand over any document the same visit.
  • Call the program or utility yourself, using the number printed on your own bill or on the program's official website — never a number the visitor gives you.
  • Ask the program directly: "Did you send someone to my door today?" A real program will tell you plainly.
  • Never hand over a Notice of Assessment, a void cheque, or full banking details to someone who approached you first.
  • If financing is involved, ask for the total loan amount, interest rate, and monthly payment in writing — and read it before the rebate is factored in, not after.

Applying through us doesn't require a door-to-door visit. Every program listed on HomePowerRebate can be verified directly on the official utility or government website linked from our pages — you never need to take a stranger's word for it.

If you think you've already been targeted

Contact your local police non-emergency line to report it, and contact the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. If you signed a financing agreement, most provinces have a short cancellation window for door-to-door sales contracts — check your provincial consumer protection office immediately, since these windows are often just a matter of days.
