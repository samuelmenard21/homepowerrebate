Seattle Just Retired Its 2,000th Oil Furnace

A city-wide program just hit a real milestone: 2,000 homes switched off oil heat. Here's what those homeowners are actually paying now, and how the math works for yours.

By Sam Menard · September 2026 · 5 min read

The milestone

Seattle's Clean Heat program just crossed a real number: 2,000 oil-heated homes have switched to electric heat pumps through the city's rebate program. That's not a marketing estimate, it's a count of actual completed installs, tracked by the city as it works toward an oil-free housing stock.

What makes this worth writing about isn't the milestone itself. It's that 2,000 households already made this decision, and their bills tell you exactly what to expect if you're considering the same move.

Why oil heat is the easiest upgrade to justify

Of every fuel switch a homeowner can make, oil-to-electric is usually the clearest win. Oil is one of the most expensive ways to heat a home, so replacing it removes the single biggest cost on your energy bill, not a marginal one.

Households that switch off oil heat typically cut their heating costs by more than half. Depending on home size, local climate, and how much oil the old furnace burned through, that works out to somewhere between $1,100 and $4,500 saved per year.

Why this is a good problem to solve first: unlike windows or high-end insulation upgrades, oil-to-heat-pump savings are large enough to notice in a single winter, not spread thin across decades.

What "switching" actually looks like

You don't need to rip out your old system on day one. Most homeowners install a heat pump as the primary heat source and keep the oil furnace as backup for the coldest days, then phase it out once they're confident in the new system. That lowers the upfront decision pressure: you're not betting your whole winter on one appliance switch.

What to ask before you commit

  • Get a load calculation, not a guess. A properly sized heat pump for your specific home is what makes the savings real. An undersized unit will underperform and an oversized one wastes money upfront.
  • Ask what happens on the coldest day of the year. A good installer will tell you exactly how the system handles your area's coldest average temperature, and whether backup heat kicks in.
  • Check what rebates apply to your area before you sign anything. Oil-to-electric conversions often qualify for larger rebates than a standard heat pump swap, because utilities and governments specifically want oil off the grid.

The bigger picture

Seattle isn't unique here. Canada's federal Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program is running the same math nationally, and cities across BC, Ontario, and the Northeast US are seeing the same pattern: oil-heated homes are consistently the fastest, most confident conversions homeowners make once they see the real numbers.

If you're homeowner #2,001 somewhere else, the path is the same one those 2,000 Seattle households already walked: get a proper quote, confirm your local rebates, and size the system to your actual home.
