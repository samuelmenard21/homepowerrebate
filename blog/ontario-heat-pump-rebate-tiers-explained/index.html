Why your Ontario heat pump rebate might be $2,000 or $12,000

Same program, same province — a six-times difference depending on two questions. Here's how to figure out where you land.

By Sam Menard · Updated August 2026 · 4 min read

Unlike BC, where the heat pump rebate scales with your household income, Ontario's Home Renovation Savings Program scales the rebate by two completely different questions: what fuel are you switching from, and are you installing an air-source or ground-source system? Get the framing right and the number makes sense immediately.

Question 1: What are you switching from?

The program splits homeowners into two groups:

  • Enbridge Gas customers currently heating with natural gas — the lower tier
  • Everyone else — electric baseboards, oil, propane, or wood heat — the higher tier

The logic: gas furnaces are already relatively efficient and lower-emission than oil, propane, or electric resistance heat, so the program pushes harder (with a bigger incentive) to get homes off those fuels first.

Question 2: Air-source or ground-source?

Air-source heat pumps (the common wall-mounted or ducted units, pulling heat from outdoor air) are cheaper to install but less efficient in extreme cold. Ground-source (geothermal) systems cost far more upfront but perform consistently regardless of outdoor temperature — and the rebate reflects that cost gap.

✅ What "per ton" means

The rebate is calculated per ton of rated heating capacity at 8.3°C (47°F) — a standard cold-climate rating point. A typical Toronto home needs a 2–3 ton system, so most households land somewhere in the middle of their tier's range, not at the maximum cap.

What about renting a heat pump instead of buying?

Heat pump rentals are eligible too, at the lower rate regardless of your current fuel — up to $2,000 for air-source, $3,000 for ground-source. If you're not ready to commit to ownership, this is worth comparing against a purchase plus the higher-tier rebate.

A worked example

Say you're in a Toronto home currently heating with a gas furnace, and you install a 2.5-ton cold-climate air-source heat pump:

  • You're an Enbridge Gas customer switching from gas — lower tier
  • 2.5 tons × $500/ton = $1,250 (under the $2,000 cap, so you get the full $1,250)

Now compare a household with electric baseboard heat installing the same 2.5-ton system:

  • Switching from electric — higher tier
  • 2.5 tons × $1,250/ton = $3,125

Same equipment, same city, 2.5x the rebate — purely because of the current fuel.

How to check which model qualifies

There's no pre-approved brand list. Instead, the heat pump must appear on Natural Resources Canada's qualified products list for either cold-climate air-source or ground-source heat pumps. Ask any participating HVAC contractor to confirm the specific model is listed before you commit — it's a quick check on their end.
