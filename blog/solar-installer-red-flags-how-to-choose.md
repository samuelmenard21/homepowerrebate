## Most BC homeowners don't know the questions to ask.

You Google "solar installers near me," see 5 companies with good reviews, call them all, get quotes, and... freeze. They all sound competent. You don't know what separates a solid installer from someone who'll leave you with an incomplete job or a forgotten Peak Saver enrollment window.

This is how people end up losing $3,500 on missed rebate deadlines, overpaying for equipment, or signing bad financing deals.

Here's what to look for—and what to avoid.

## Red flag #1: "We can start immediately"

A solid installer has a 3–6 week backlog. They're busy. They're installing multiple systems per week in your region.

An installer who says "we can start next week" either has no other business or is cutting corners. Either way, that's a warning.

**Why this matters:** Good installers are selective about quality control. They book out because every job gets proper electrical engineering review and structural assessment. Installers with zero backlog often rush through these, leading to:
- Missed permitting steps (system can't be energized)
- Incomplete Peak Saver enrollment (you lose the $3,500 battery rebate)
- Weak after-installation support (when something goes wrong, they're gone)

**What to ask:** "What's your typical timeline from contract to activation?" If they say 3+ weeks and have a backlog of customers in your region, that's a good sign.

## Red flag #2: They can't name their HPCN certification

As of June 1, 2026, only HPCN-certified contractors can claim BC Hydro rebates. This is non-negotiable.

If an installer says:
- "We're HPCN" but can't give you a certificate number or company registration
- "We'll get certified before your installation"
- "The certification doesn't matter if we document it differently"

**Run.** They're either lying or cutting corners on compliance. Either way, your rebate is at risk.

**What to ask:** "Can you show me your current HPCN certification?" Get the certificate number and verify it at the [HPCN registry](https://hpcn.ca). Takes 2 minutes.

## Red flag #3: Vague warranty language

A solar panel comes with a 20–25 year manufacturer warranty. An inverter has a 10–15 year warranty. The *installation* (workmanship) should have its own warranty—usually 10 years minimum.

An installer who gives you a 1-year warranty or says "the manufacturer covers it" is signaling that they don't stand behind the installation quality.

**Why this matters:** If an electrical connection fails (not the panel, but the installer's wiring), the panel manufacturer won't cover it. Your installer should.

**Red flags:**
- "1-year workmanship warranty" (should be 5–10)
- Warranty document doesn't exist or is vague
- Warranty covers "parts only" and not labor

**What to ask:** "What's your workmanship warranty, and does it cover both parts and labor?" Get it in writing. A solid installer will say "10 years, parts and labor."

## Red flag #4: They don't explain the Peak Saver 14-day window

This is the #1 gotcha in BC right now. Battery rebate is $5,000 *only if* Peak Saver enrollment happens within 14 days of interconnection approval.

If an installer:
- Doesn't mention Peak Saver at all
- Says "it's optional"
- Says "we'll handle it eventually"
- Can't explain the 14-day clock

**That's a red flag.** They either don't understand the rebate structure or they're not tracking their own handover process.

**What to ask:** "Will you enroll my battery in Peak Saver within 14 days of BC Hydro interconnection approval? Can you write that into the contract?"

A good installer will put it in writing and give you a timeline. A bad installer will wave it off as "we'll figure it out later."

## Red flag #5: They pressure you on financing before you understand the economics

You should understand the cost and payback *before* signing financing paperwork.

**Red flags:**
- "Let me get you a deal I can't offer tomorrow" (artificial urgency)
- "Sign today and lock in this rate" (pressuring you)
- "0% for 5 years, no problem" (sounds too good to be true, probably is)
- They avoid questions about the actual payback math

Bad financing often hides high interest rates after the promotional period, inflated equipment costs, or terms that make it hard to leave.

**What to ask:** 
- "What's the total cost broken down by component (panels, inverter, battery, labor)?"
- "If I pay cash instead of financing, what's the actual cost?"
- "What are the real interest rates after the promotional period?"

A solid installer should hand you a detailed quote with equipment specs and can explain the payback without selling you financing.

## Red flag #6: They don't have local references

A good installer has 20+ completed systems in your city. They can give you the names of actual homeowners you can call.

**Red flags:**
- "We don't share references" (why not?)
- "All our recent installs are in other cities" (so they're new to your area?)
- They get defensive when you ask for references

**What to ask:** "Can you give me 3 references from completed systems in my city in the last 12 months? I want to call them and ask about your communication and follow-up."

Call those references. Ask:
- "Did they finish on time?"
- "Was the communication clear throughout?"
- "Do they respond to questions after the installation?"
- "Would you use them again?"

## Red flag #7: They can't explain the technology

An installer should be able to explain:
- How net metering works (solar sends power to grid, you get credit)
- How Peak Saver works (battery discharges to grid during peak demand, you get paid)
- How battery efficiency works (round-trip losses, why a battery can't store 100% of what you send)
- Why Tesla Powerwall doesn't qualify for BC rebates (not on approved list)

If they can't explain these clearly, they don't understand the product they're selling. That's dangerous.

**What to ask:** "Walk me through how the battery will work during a winter outage—what can I run and for how long?" They should be able to explain actual usage scenarios, not just specs.
