Water Heater Buying Guide for California Homes

California doesn't run one water heater rebate. It runs five, one per utility, and they aren't close to each other in size. SMUD customers can get $4,000. SDG&E customers get $250 for the same unit.

This guide covers the three real options, how to size each one, and what your specific utility actually pays.

By Sam Menard · Updated August 2026 · 10 min read

Your Utility Sets the Rebate, Not the State

Every California utility runs its own water heater incentive, and the gap between them is large:

UtilityTerritoryRebate (2026)
SMUDSacramentoUp to $4,000
LADWPLos AngelesUp to $2,500
PG&EBay AreaAround $500
SDG&ESan DiegoAround $250

SMUD is a municipal utility and runs its own budget separate from the investor-owned utilities, which is a large part of why its incentive is so much bigger. Check your specific city's water heater page on this site for the confirmed current figure before you budget around any of these.

The Three Types, Straight Up

Standard Tank (Gas or Electric)

Cost installed: $1,200-$2,000. Lifespan: 8-12 years. Cheapest upfront, and typically not eligible for any of the rebates above, which target heat pump models specifically.

Tankless (On-Demand)

Cost installed: $2,500-$4,500. Lifespan: 15-20 years. Popular in California's many slab-foundation, no-attic homes where a tank takes up space nobody wants to give up. Most tankless models don't qualify for the heat pump rebate.

Heat Pump (Hybrid)

Cost installed: $2,500-$4,500 before rebate. Lifespan: 10-15 years. This is the category every California utility rebate targets. In SMUD or LADWP territory, the rebate covers most or all of the price gap over a standard tank. In SDG&E territory, where the rebate is smallest and electricity rates are the highest in the state, run the payback numbers before you commit.

Sizing It Right

Tank sizing (by household size)

  • 1-2 people: 30-40 gallons
  • 3-4 people: 40-50 gallons
  • 5+ people: 60-80 gallons

Tankless sizing (by flow rate)

Size to your peak simultaneous demand, not average use: shower, kitchen tap, and dishwasher running at the same time. Many California homes built before 1978 have minimal insulation, which raises incoming water temperature swings and makes correct sizing matter more than the spec sheet suggests.

Who Qualifies

  • You're a residential customer of the utility offering the rebate
  • You're replacing an existing unit with an ENERGY STAR-certified heat pump water heater
  • An eligible installer or retailer processes the rebate, depending on your utility's specific process

What to Ask Before You Sign

  1. "Is this model on my specific utility's qualifying list?" (SMUD, LADWP, PG&E, and SDG&E each maintain separate lists.)
  2. "Is the rebate instant at checkout, or a mail-in claim after install?"
  3. "What's the real installed cost after my utility's specific rebate?"
  4. "Where will this vent, and does the space have enough airflow?"
If you're near a utility boundary, confirm which one actually serves your address before assuming your neighbor's rebate applies to you. Some California cities sit close enough to a territory line that two houses a few blocks apart answer to different utilities with very different incentives.

The One Number That Matters

A heat pump water heater uses roughly a third of the electricity of a standard electric tank. In SMUD or LADWP territory, that plus the rebate typically brings payback to 3-5 years. In PG&E or SDG&E territory, where the rebate is smaller, payback stretches closer to 6-9 years, still worth doing, just a longer horizon.
