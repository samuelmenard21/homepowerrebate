HRV & Ventilation Rebates in Prince George

Prince George homeowners can get up to $1,600 toward a heat recovery ventilator through CleanBC's income-qualified Energy Savings Program — but only when it's paired with a heat pump, insulation, or windows upgrade.

The honest answer: no standalone HRV rebate in Prince George

There is no standalone BC HRV or ERV rebate. The provincial CleanBC Better Homes Energy Savings Program pays up to $1,600 toward a heat or energy recovery ventilator (and up to $300 for a bathroom fan), but only for income-qualified households (Level 1 or Level 2), and only when the ventilation work is installed alongside a qualifying heat pump, heat pump water heater, insulation, or windows/doors upgrade in the same project. Income Level 3 households and anyone installing ventilation on its own get nothing toward it.

Prince George shares the northern fossil-fuel-switching top-up with Fort St. John, aimed at homes moving off wood or oil heat, and ventilation only qualifies for a rebate when it's part of that same switch-and-upgrade project.

Who actually qualifies

  • Your household falls into Income Level 1 or Level 2 under the Energy Savings Program's income thresholds (based on household size and combined pre-tax income)
  • You have a residential BC Hydro account at the Prince George service address
  • The HRV or ERV unit is on the program's qualified product list, installed by a Registered Contractor
  • The ventilation work is submitted in the same application as a qualifying heat pump, heat pump water heater, insulation, or windows/doors upgrade

What doesn't qualify

A ventilation-only project in Prince George — installing an HRV or ERV with nothing else happening — is not eligible for any rebate under this program, regardless of income. Income Level 3 households (the higher-income tier of BetterHomes BC's Energy Savings Program) also receive nothing toward ventilation, even when it's paired with a qualifying upgrade.

Why homes end up needing HRV anyway

Sealing a Prince George home for a heat pump or insulation upgrade makes it airtight enough that stale air, moisture, and indoor pollutants stop escaping on their own. An HRV or ERV swaps that stale air for fresh outdoor air while recovering most of the heat, which is why the province ties the rebate to a heat pump or envelope project rather than paying for it as a stand-alone comfort upgrade. Understanding your HRV or ERV

An HRV exchanges stale indoor air for fresh outdoor air while recovering most of the heat that would otherwise leave with it, using a heat-exchange core to transfer that warmth into the incoming air before it reaches your living space. Natural Resources Canada's guide makes the case that this is close to essential in a northern BC home sealed tightly enough for insulation or a heat pump upgrade, since that airtightness also traps moisture and indoor pollutants that an older, leakier house would have vented on its own.

Prince George's winters run cold enough, and dry enough, that an ERV is usually the more sensible choice over a plain HRV, since it recovers some humidity along with heat rather than exhausting all of it, helping avoid the uncomfortably dry indoor air that can show up in a sealed northern home. Realistic recovery efficiency in Prince George's moderate-cold range runs around 60-75%; the city's 10-year average January low sits near -9.7°C, and on the genuinely coldest nights recovery drops further while the unit may run extended defrost cycles to keep the core from icing over, adding downtime.

Homeowners here regularly ask if it's worth running the HRV through a long Prince George winter when the incoming air feels cold. It is: the unit has already recovered most of the usable heat from your exhaust air, so what you're feeling is the smaller remaining share, not the full outdoor temperature. Turning the unit off completely in a tightly sealed home instead risks trapped humidity, window condensation, and mold, a bigger problem over a northern winter than the partial cost of conditioning outdoor air. It's not a bathroom fan replacement either; keep dedicated exhaust running there since HRV ducting can't clear steam fast enough. Check Natural Resources Canada's ENERGY STAR certified HRV/ERV products list before buying, since cold-climate performance varies between models.

How to claim it in Prince George

  1. Check what you qualify for first with the free rebate assessment — it'll tell you if a heat pump, insulation, or windows project is realistic for your home
  2. Confirm your household income falls into Level 1 or Level 2 using the Energy Savings Program's household-size thresholds
  3. Book a Registered Contractor for the paired project (heat pump, water heater, insulation, or windows) and have them include the HRV/ERV or bathroom fan in the same scope
  4. The contractor submits the rebate application and supporting documentation on your behalf within six months of the invoice date

Is there a standalone HRV rebate in Prince George?

Why ventilation matters more in Prince George's winters

Prince George's average January daily low over the last 10 winters (2015-2024) has been -9.7°C, with a coldest recorded day of -42.4°C. Colder outdoor air holds less moisture, which is exactly why a well-sealed Prince George home needs mechanical ventilation in the first place — without an HRV, that sealed-up house traps humidity indoors instead of exchanging stale air for fresh air without losing the heat you paid for.
